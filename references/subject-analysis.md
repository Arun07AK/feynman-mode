# Analyse the subject first

Do this before designing anything. The output is a one-page learning design that the learner approves.

## What to work out

From the syllabus, slides and past papers, answer three questions.

1. **What kind of knowledge is this, and what does the exam make the learner do with it?** Most subjects mix several kinds, so answer per module where they differ.
2. **Where are the hard parts, and what makes them hard?** Name the specific ideas, not the chapters.
3. **So what follows?** How topics should be split, what "done" means for a topic, what practice should look like, and how much teaching each part of the assessment deserves.

## Kinds of knowledge, and what usually follows

Use this as a starting point for question 3, not as a lookup table. Where the subject's own evidence disagrees, follow the evidence.

| The exam asks the learner to | Split topics by | A topic is done when the learner can | Practice looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calculate (numericals, algorithms traced by hand) | one procedure or formula family per topic | solve a fresh problem of each exam type unaided | full worked attempts on paper, checked step by step |
| Prove or derive | one result and the ideas it needs | reproduce the argument and justify each step | writing the derivation from a blank page |
| Design or model (diagrams, architectures, schemas) | one design decision or notation per topic | produce a design for a new case and defend the choices | open tasks compared with a model answer |
| Write code | one construct or technique per topic | write and debug a small program unaided | writing and running code, not reading it |
| Recall and explain (definitions, comparisons, short notes) | clusters of related ideas | explain each idea and tell near neighbours apart | answering from memory, then comparing with the source |
| Argue or analyse (cases, essays) | one framework or theme per topic | apply the framework to an unseen case | timed written answers |

## Things that change with the subject

- **Topic size.** A heavy idea gets its own topic; light ones merge. A calculation subject tends to have fewer, deeper topics; a recall subject has more, shallower ones.
- **The teaching template.** The default template (`assets/teaching-template.txt`) has a rule for proofs and numerical problems. For a subject with neither, the pilot thread is the test of whether it still teaches well. If it does not, propose a change to the learner. Do not edit the template silently.
- **Lectures.** In some subjects a strong course exists for everything; in others good teaching is scattered or missing. Say what you expect before searching.
- **Practice volume.** Follow the weightage. A topic that never appears in papers still gets taught properly, but gets little practice.
- **Prior study.** Topics the learner has already studied open with a short diagnostic and teach only what is shaky.

## The one-page learning design

Show the learner:

- the kinds of knowledge found, per module, with one line of evidence each (a syllabus line or a past question);
- the hard parts, and why each is hard;
- the proposed split: modules and topics in study order, with rough sizes;
- what "done" will mean, and what practice will look like;
- how this differs from a generic plan, in two or three lines;
- anything unclear that needs their decision.

If something essential is missing (exam date and scope, what they have studied, which decisions are theirs), interview them before writing the page. After their go, work through to completion and come back only for decisions that need them.
